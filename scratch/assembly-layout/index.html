AlphaNIST

Assembly has a clear goal, but many possible solutions. In simulation, AlphaNIST learns to grasp, reorient, and assemble parts using binary task rewards, without demonstrations or prescribed manipulation stages.

The assembly challenge

The NIST board combines twenty assemblies, from nut threading and gear meshing to peg and connector insertion. Parts as small as 4 mm in diameter must be grasped, reoriented, and aligned with tight-fitting mating surfaces.

Small errors can cause slipping or jamming, requiring the robot to recover and try again. With several components on the board, it must also handle clutter and choose an assembly order: placing one part can obstruct access to another.

Learning setup

We ask how much dexterity and coordination can be learned from a reward for the completed task. In the single-component experiments, one shared policy learns across twenty NIST assembly tasks.

Training combines cached starting states, beta sampling, and large-scale parallel experience. The task reward is binary: completed assembly receives a reward; reaching, grasping, and partial insertion do not receive progress rewards.

Multi-component policies are trained separately from the single-component policy. Here, the task reward requires all active parts to be assembled. Neither the assembly order nor the individual maneuvers are prescribed.

Starting states

A cached bank contains physically valid starting states: loose parts, in-hand grasps, and partial assemblies.

Beta sampling puts more training on states the policy sometimes solves, while retaining a chance to sample every state. These are starting states, not demonstrations of how to finish.

Training across assemblies

With binary task rewards, one shared policy reaches higher training success than twenty separately trained specialists. In this comparison, adding the tested dense reward shaping reduces success, especially for shared training.

Observed assembly behaviors

The robot sets down a center-held peg and grasps nearer its end for insertion. It uses the board to reorient a connector, and turns nuts through repeated fingertip contacts.

Learning dexterity and coordination

Training for completed assembly produces both precise manipulation and choices about how to organize longer sequences. The policies learn grasp changes, move obstructing parts, and adjust their motions around neighboring components, without prescribed manipulation stages.

In these experiments, shared training gives higher training success than separate specialists, and binary task rewards outperform the dense shaping tested here. The multi-component examples show that the same kind of objective can also support sequences in which the policy chooses what to assemble next.

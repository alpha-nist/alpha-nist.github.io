What does it take for that to happen in robotics?

The assembly challenge

The NIST board combines twenty assembly tasks, with parts as small as 4 mm. A single assembly may require repeated changes of grasp and orientation before insertion is possible. Tight clearances and friction make contact difficult to control: a slight tilt can cause a nut to cross-thread or jam instead of advancing along the bolt. Parts can also slip during manipulation, requiring the robot to recover and try again.

With several components, each assembly changes the conditions for the next. Installing one part can obstruct access to another or leave less room for the gripper to move. The robot may need to bring a part within reach, leave it unfinished, and return after assembling something else. These decisions have consequences much later in the sequence, while the task reward is withheld until every active component is assembled.

Giving robots room to be creative

Trying to prescribe every maneuver means anticipating how the robot should respond as contacts and obstructions change. A two-finger gripper may also need solutions quite different from those that come naturally to a human hand.

Perhaps the answer is to stop telling the robot how we think the task should be done, and let it learn how to get there. We give it a binary task reward for success and train it through vast amounts of diverse experience. That leaves room for the robot to discover solutions we might never have thought to teach.

Starting states

A robot learning from loose parts may rarely reach the insertion step at first. We give it practice from a wider range of configurations, including parts already grasped or partly assembled. These states are generated in simulation and saved for reuse.

Starting near completion lets the robot encounter successful assemblies while it is still learning to handle loose parts. Each saved state specifies where an attempt begins, not which actions should follow.

Success-guided sampling

As learning progresses, some starting states become easy while others remain out of reach. Success-guided sampling uses the outcomes of recent attempts to adjust how often each state is revisited.

We use beta sampling, which gives the greatest weight to states the policy solves about half the time. Every state retains a chance of being sampled. Practice elsewhere can also make a previously unsuccessful state easier; when the robot returns to it, new outcomes update its sampling weight.

Training across assemblies

We compare one policy trained across all twenty tasks with twenty policies trained separately. With binary task rewards, the shared policy reaches higher training success. Adding rewards for reaching and alignment lowers success in this comparison, particularly for the shared policy.

Observed assembly behaviors

The robot sometimes puts a part down before trying to insert it. For an 8 mm peg, it releases a grasp at the center and picks the peg up near one end, leaving more of it exposed. Other sequences show a connector turned against the board and a small nut threaded with repeated fingertip taps. These maneuvers were not demonstrated or assigned their own rewards.

Learning dexterity and coordination

In the ten-component runs, the robot sometimes brings a connector within reach, assembles another part, and then returns to the connector. It also uses short turns when threading a nut beside an assembled Ethernet connector, avoiding contact between its hand and the connector.

The task reward requires every active part to be assembled, but does not specify their order. The policy fits individual parts and adjusts its actions as the board fills up.
